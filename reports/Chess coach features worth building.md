# Close the "why" gap before adding models

Players rated roughly 200-1200 improve most from what Prophylaxis already does at its core: making them retry their own decisive mistakes, bringing those positions back on a spaced schedule, and giving a short, correct reason. The weakest link is the reason. In the sample player's games, **56 of 82 mistakes get no named cause**. On chess.com beginner blunders, **125 of 385 match none of the 15 tactic themes**. And "tell me *why*" is the request players repeat most often about game review. AI adds real value in one place that rules and Stockfish cannot reach: predicting what a human at a given rating would play. **Maia-3 does this in a 10.8 MB browser-ready model**, which lets the coach say "this was easy for someone at your level" or "this is the usual mistake at your rating" instead of judging every move against perfect play. Language models are the main gimmick risk. Even a frontier model gets **22% of its chess claims wrong without tools**, small open models get **more than 40% wrong**, and only small models fit in a browser. So any LLM should only reword facts the engine has already checked, or not be used at all. The evidence is thinner than the market suggests: **no controlled trial of any chess training method exists for this rating band**. These priorities therefore rest on general learning science (strong), platform correlations (moderate) and consistent forum demand (weak per item). Prophylaxis needs local, opt-in measurement to confirm them. The recommended order is: rule-and-engine explanations for non-tactical mistakes first, then a Maia-3 difficulty layer, then an upgrade from the current spaced-repetition scheduler to FSRS (a newer scheduler with better benchmark results). Compare and the opening tree should be frozen, not expanded.

## Strong evidence comes from learning science, not chess studies

The chess literature cannot rank training activities for beginners. Across studies of rated players, accumulated **serious solo study was the strongest single predictor of rating**. Deliberate practice explains only **about 34% of the variance** in skill once measurement error is corrected ([Burgoyne et al. 2019](https://englelab.gatech.edu/articles/2019/Burgoyne_et_al_2019_AJP.pdf)). The hours needed to reach master ranged **from 3,016 to 23,608**, about an eightfold spread ([Gobet & Campitelli 2007](https://core.ac.uk/download/334665.pdf)). These studies are retrospective, correlational, and drawn from club players and above. None isolates puzzles, own-game review or opening study within "serious study". One finding does apply directly to Prophylaxis's audience: the link between cognitive ability and chess skill is **stronger in young and lower-rated players** ([Burgoyne et al. 2016](https://www.psychologie.uzh.ch/dam/jcr:4b13cd49-3e58-409f-988e-1e73693e1e0b/Burgoyne.Intelligence.2016.pdf)). That supports short explanations that ask the player to hold one idea at a time.

Puzzle evidence is correlational or comes from vendors. Lichess puzzle rating correlates **0.74 with classical rating across 71,820 players** ([Lichess forum](https://lichess.org/forum/general-chess-discussion/low-correlation-between-puzzle-proficiency--rating-implications-for-chess-improvement)), but stronger players solving harder puzzles explains much of that. The only longitudinal signal is a vendor's analysis of 431,000 Woodpecker-method puzzles. It found about **+35 Elo**, smaller gains above 1600, and **larger gains for users who repeated the same set** ([DiscoChess on Lichess](https://lichess.org/@/DiscoChessApp/blog/woodpecker-method-training-431000-puzzles-60000-rated-games-and-35-elo/yrWsFF1N)). The vendor itself says this does not prove causation. A study of 1,256 Lichess players found that **starting rating strongly predicts later rating change** ([JEI](https://emerginginvestigators.org/articles/25-024/pdf)), so any naive before-and-after comparison of Prophylaxis users will be distorted by regression to the mean.

The controlled evidence comes from general learning science, and it backs Prophylaxis's existing design. Dunlosky's review rated **practice testing and distributed practice the only two "high utility" techniques of ten** ([APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/which-study-strategies-make-the-grade.html)). Attempting an answer before seeing it helps **even when the attempt fails** ([Kornell, Hays & Bjork 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19586265/); [Richland, Kornell & Kao 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19751074/)). The benefit is not universal, though: failed attempts followed by immediate feedback sometimes impair learning ([Knight et al. 2012](https://amalab.uta.edu/files/2021/12/knight-2012-2.pdf)), so failure should stay cheap and be corrected quickly. Feedback averages **d = 0.48 across 435 studies**, and more informative feedback works better ([Wisniewski, Zierer & Hattie 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03087/full)). Worked examples help novices and then stop helping as skill grows ([Kalyuga 2007](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007%2Fs10648-007-9054-3)), which argues for explaining a motif fully the first time and then fading to "find it yourself". Progress monitoring raises goal attainment by **d = 0.40 across 138 randomised experiments**, mostly in health. In those trials, monitoring a behaviour changed the behaviour but not the outcome, and monitoring the outcome did the reverse ([Harkin et al. 2016](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/87431/)). Prophylaxis should therefore track both a habit rate and rating.

Coaching advice fills the remaining gaps. Heisman's routine of checking the opponent's checks, captures and threats is the standard prescription below club level ([New in Chess excerpt](https://www.newinchess.com/media/wysiwyg/product_pdf/7631.pdf)). Coaches note that **beginners attend to their own ideas and neglect the opponent's** ([ChessKid](https://www.chesskid.com/learn/articles/checks-captures-queen-attacks)). No study shows either point reduces blunders. Opening study divides coaches: some say beginners should learn only the first few moves, others say that advice breeds frustration.

| Claim relevant to Prophylaxis | Strength | Design consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Retrieval and spacing improve learning | Controlled, general (not chess) | Keep "find the move first" and spaced review as the core loop |
| Feedback helps; richer feedback helps more | Meta-analysis, general | Every resurfaced mistake needs a reason, not only a better move |
| Worked examples fade in value with skill | Controlled, mostly maths/physics | Full explanation on first encounter with a motif, shorter after |
| Monitoring behaviour changes behaviour | 138 RCTs, mostly health | Track habit rate per game alongside rating |
| Puzzle repetition goes with modest gains below about 1600 | Vendor observational | Repeat the user's own missed positions; avoid promising Elo gains |
| Hanging pieces and missed forcing moves decide low-rated games | Expert consensus | Opponent-threat detection belongs at the centre |
| Openings matter little below about 1500 | Expert opinion, disputed | Keep opening features light |

## Players ask for "why", and chess.com charges most for it

What players want from review is consistent, though the evidence is mostly forum posts. The clearest wish is a reason. One chess.com user wrote that Game Review "doesn't tell you why, and that's more important than just knowing the best move" ([chess.com forum](https://www.chess.com/forum/view/game-analysis/is-there-a-way-to-see-why-something-is-a-good-bad-move)). Others complain that engine lines run **"like a 12 move long sequence"** that no human would find ([chess.com forum](https://www.chess.com/forum/view/community/anybody-find-that-the-game-review-makes-no-sense-sometimes)). Players also want honest rather than flattering labels: many threads attack "Brilliant" moves that are not brilliant ([chess.com forum](https://www.chess.com/forum/view/site-feedback/brilliant-moves-arent-all-that-brilliant)). And they want to know what to study next, a request that recurs in "stuck at 800" threads ([chess.com forum](https://www.chess.com/forum/view/general/im-hard-stuck-800-what-should-i-do)). Lichess users repeatedly ask for "Learn from your mistakes" to work across all their games and to use spaced repetition ([Lichess forum](https://lichess.org/forum/lichess-feedback/create-learn-from-your-mistakes-across-all-games)). These are single threads, and Reddit could not be searched, so the ordering of these wants is unknown. The fact that each one recurs is reliable.

The only large survey comes from chess.com's 2025 improvement challenge (**n = 33,131**). In it, **94% had a rating goal**, 56% wanted better openings and **44% wanted better study habits** ([chess.com](https://www.chess.com/article/view/what-a-chess-improver-looks-like)). The respondents averaged 1153, against a site rapid average of 616, so the survey describes motivated improvers above Prophylaxis's median user. The 44% figure suggests consistency, not intent, is the bottleneck.

The market leaves these needs unmet for free users. Free chess.com members get **one full Game Review per day**. Per-move coach explanations and Advanced Stats require Diamond, the top tier ([chess.com support](https://support.chess.com/en/articles/8562418-what-does-each-level-of-premium-membership-get-me)). The cross-game Takeaways and Report Card added in July 2026 are also complete only for Diamond ([chess.com changelog](https://www.chess.com/blog/Product/whats-new-on-chess-com)). Aimchess, the closest aggregate-weakness product, allows **one 40-game report per month** on its free tier ([aimchess.com](https://aimchess.com/)). LLM coach apps ration explanations with credits, because each call costs money ([Chessiro comparison](https://chessiro.com/blog/best-chess-analysis-tools)). Lichess gives free unlimited analysis but no plain-language reason. Only about 2 million of chess.com's **267 million members** pay ([chess.com Q2 2026 report](https://www.chess.com/board-reports/2026-q2); [Pulse.bot/Adweek](https://www.pulse.bot/digital-marketing/news/chesscom-surpassed-250-million-users-and-is-launching-a-new-advertising-gambit-e8af1591-21c3-4e7b-abff-3eb21dc09ca1/)), so the free-and-private gap is large. None of the reviewed products markets privacy. Prophylaxis's position is unusual and defensible: free, unlimited, cross-game, no account. Competing on celebrity voices, million-puzzle libraries or bot catalogues would not be.

## Machine learning earns its place where rules and Stockfish go silent

ML is the right tool only when it supplies something deterministic code cannot. Stockfish knows the best move. Rules know whether a piece was left undefended. Neither knows what a 700-rated human would see, and that is where the strongest ML evidence sits. Position difficulty predicts human error **more strongly than player skill or time** ([Anderson, Kleinberg & Mullainathan 2016](https://arxiv.org/abs/1606.04956)). Maia models predict human moves conditioned on rating. Maia-3's model sizes (3M to 79M parameters) match human moves between **54.8% and 57.1%** of the time, against 52.0% for Maia-2 ([Chessformer/Maia-3 paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.19091)). Community ONNX exports put the 5M model at **10.8 MB in fp16**, runnable in onnxruntime-web ([HF bqrio/maia3-onnx](https://huggingface.co/bqrio/maia3-onnx)), and maiachess.com already runs Maia and Stockfish together in the browser ([maia-platform-frontend](https://github.com/CSSLab/maia-platform-frontend)). Prophylaxis already loads onnxruntime-web for its tactic model, so the 14 MB runtime cost is already paid and Maia would add only its own weights. Three things are unknown. No browser latency figures are published. Maia-3 was trained on Lichess blitz from 600 to 2600 Elo ([Lichess blog](https://lichess.org/@/ashtonanderson/blog/introducing-maia-3-free-and-open-source/vCPPRtX3)), so chess.com ratings need mapping, and some sub-600 users may sit outside the training range. And its accuracy on rapid and daily games is unchecked.

Blunder prediction shows the limits of a model that judges a single position. From the board alone, a residual CNN predicted whether a human would blunder next with **67.7% accuracy**, rising to **71.7% with metadata** ([Maia paper](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2006.01855)). That beats chance but is not trustworthy enough to show a user one position at a time. Puzzle-difficulty prediction is a mature benchmark: the IEEE BigData Cup 2024 drew almost 100 teams, and the FedCSIS 2025 winner fed **Maia-2 plus Leela and Stockfish features into gradient-boosted trees** ([FedCSIS 2025](https://annals-csis.org/Volume_43/drp/6497.html)). That recipe is trainable on a laptop from the CC0 puzzle set ([database.lichess.org](https://database.lichess.org/)). However, the winners' error in rating points could not be retrieved, and no study tests whether these models transfer from curated puzzles to positions from real games.

Tactic classification is where Prophylaxis already has ML, and it has a structural ceiling. Lichess puzzles are tagged automatically and refined by votes ([database.lichess.org](https://database.lichess.org/)), and they are chosen for having a forcing solution. A classifier trained on them can only ever name tactics. It cannot explain the 56 unexplained mistakes if most of them are not tactical. Prophylaxis's own numbers show it working where it applies (macro F1 0.91 on puzzles, F1 ≥ 0.80 on every label it shows for sub-1000 chess.com blunders). They also show discipline: the pin label was cut when a person accepted only about a third of its pins. The research notes infer that the Lichess tagger is rule-based. If so, the classifier mostly reproduces hand-written rules and could in principle be replaced by porting them. The tagger lives in an AGPL-3.0 repository ([lichess-puzzler](https://github.com/ornicar/lichess-puzzler)), and whether it is purely rule-based is unverified.

Natural-language generation is where AI is most likely to be a gimmick. A 2026 tool-augmented evaluation found GPT-5.4 got **22.0% of its chess sub-claims wrong** without tools, and smaller open-weight models **over 40%**. It also found that LLM-as-judge metrics missed these errors: one commentary scored 4.9 out of 5 while two of its three main claims were false ([Hebbar et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.04240); [alphaXiv](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2608.04240)). Grounding helps. Concept-guided commentary that listed every real attack before generating was rated accurate by human judges ([Kim et al., NAACL 2025](https://aclanthology.org/2025.naacl-long.481)). A browser can host only roughly 0.4-3.5 GB models through WebLLM ([web-llm config](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/mlc-ai/web-llm/main/src/config.ts)). Chrome's built-in Gemini Nano requires desktop Chrome 148+, **more than 4 GB VRAM or 16 GB RAM, and 22 GB of free disk**, and is not available on Android or iOS ([Chrome Prompt API](https://developer.chrome.com/docs/ai/prompt-api.md.txt)). A meaningful share of low-rated, often younger, players will not meet those requirements. The trustworthy architecture is: the engine and rules produce facts, a template renders them, and an LLM at most rephrases under a schema, with a validator that rejects any square or piece not in the fact list. Players' distrust of chess.com's Coach, which a prominent blogger calls "entirely unreliable" ([Duckfest](https://www.chess.com/blog/Duckfest/game-review-common-confusion)), shows the cost of getting this wrong.

Per-game rating estimation was correctly cut. Chess.com itself says its game rating "may be much higher or lower than your actual playing strength" in any one game ([chess.com support](https://support.chess.com/en/articles/10773754-how-is-game-rating-calculated-in-game-review)). It also draws many "LIE AND DECEIT" forum threads ([chess.com forum](https://www.chess.com/forum/view/general/game-review-estimated-game-rating-is-a-lie-and-deceit)). Anderson's finding that difficulty dominates error explains the noise: a quiet game produces high accuracy at any level. Prophylaxis's own test (about 300 points off, on 529 games) agrees.

Spaced-repetition scheduling is a small, well-evidenced ML upgrade. FSRS beats an adapted SM-2 on log loss in **97.4-99.6% of about 10,000 Anki collections** ([Expertium benchmark](https://expertium.github.io/Benchmark.html)). SuperMemo disputes the metric ([SuperMemo](https://supermemo.com/en/blog/supermemo-is-better-than-fsrs-by-far)), and no evidence ties it to chess.

Licensing is less of a barrier than it looks. The Prophylaxis repository is **GPL-3.0**, and it already ships Stockfish 19 lite single-threaded, which is GPL-3.0 ([stockfish.js](https://github.com/nmrugg/stockfish.js)). Maia-3's code and weights are **AGPL-3.0** ([CSSLab/maia3](https://github.com/CSSLab/maia3)). Section 13 of GPLv3 explicitly allows combining with AGPLv3 work, and the AGPL part keeps its own terms ([GNU GPLv3](https://www.gnu.org/licenses/gpl-3.0.html)). AGPL's network clause requires offering source to users who interact with the software over a network. A public repository already satisfies that. One open point: the Hugging Face model card defers to the repo for the weights' licence, so confirm before shipping. Maia-2 is **MIT** ([CSSLab/maia2](https://github.com/CSSLab/maia2)), which would matter only if Prophylaxis ever went closed-source or was relicensed permissively. Choosing it would cost about 3 points of accuracy, a larger 23M-parameter model, and building an ONNX export yourself. Maia-1 is GPL-3.0. The Lichess puzzle and game databases are CC0. Any JavaScript port of the Lichess tagger would be AGPL-licensed derivative code.

## Existing features: keep the learning loop, shrink the scoreboard

The ranking below orders current features by contribution to improvement, weighting controlled evidence above demand signals. "Keep" means keep as it is. "Improve" means a specific change backed by the evidence. "Freeze" means keep it working but make no further investment unless measurement justifies it.

Two judgements deserve explanation. First, the tactic classifier stays, but it is no longer the place to invest. Most named blunders are hanging pieces and forks that the rules already catch, so the CNN's extra coverage over the rules is small. It is also capped by training data that is tactical by construction. Its cost is now low because it loads lazily, and its precision clears the bar on every label it shows. If Maia ships, the onnxruntime-web runtime is loaded anyway and the CNN's own cost drops to about 1 MB of weights. Removing it would then save little. The honest portfolio story is measurement and pruning, not "I trained a CNN". Second, accuracy and grades stay because chess.com players expect them, but they are where competitors lose trust. Chess.com's own documentation says its classification thresholds vary with player rating ([chess.com support](https://support.chess.com/en/articles/8572705-how-are-moves-classified-what-is-a-blunder-or-brilliant-and-etc)), and users read that as flattery. Prophylaxis gains more by being conservative than by matching it.

The cuts already made should stay cut. The per-game rating estimator failed on Prophylaxis's own data and on the structural argument. The pin label failed human acceptance. Both decisions follow the right rule: a label shown to beginners must be trustworthy enough that a wrong one does not teach the wrong lesson.

Some candidates should not be built now. A **chat coach** needs a paid API and carries the highest hallucination exposure. A **Maia-fitted "you play like X ± Y" rating** across 20+ games is technically defensible: Maia4All personalises from about 20 games ([Maia4All](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.21488v1)). But users already have a real rating computed from hundreds of games, so it would rebuild the cut estimator's appeal without its noise and without a user problem. A **per-position blunder-risk warning** is not reliable enough to show one position at a time, and its only post-game use is already covered by rank 2's "hard position, fast move" flag. **Celebrity voices, large bot catalogues and giant puzzle libraries** are where chess.com and Chessiverse are already strong, and none of them answers "why".

Rank 1 comes before Maia because it attacks the measured gap with no new download and no new failure mode. Many unexplained mistakes at this level are probably "missed what the opponent was doing" or "allowed a short forcing sequence". A threat check catches both and maps directly onto Heisman's checks-captures-threats routine, so each explanation doubles as a lesson in the habit coaches recommend. That is an inference to verify on the 56 unexplained cases. Classify them by hand first. If most turn out to be quiet positional errors, the template families should change before any code is written. The success metric is the drop in unexplained mistakes on the same 82-mistake and 385-blunder sets, with precision checked by human acceptance.

Rank 2 is where Prophylaxis's AI story becomes distinctive rather than catch-up. Every competitor judges moves against Stockfish. Ranking a user's misses by how findable they were at the user's rating turns "you had a blunder" into "this was a 1-move win most 800s find; that one was a 2000-level idea you can ignore". That matches both Anderson's finding and the expertise-reversal logic of teaching what the learner can absorb. The validation plan is concrete and $0. On the existing 385 chess.com blunders, check how often Maia at the player's mapped rating assigns real probability to the blunder actually played. Then check whether "easy miss" labels agree with a human rater. Ship the labels only as coarse bands, never as precise numbers, because the error in rating points of difficulty models is not published.

## Measure locally, or the ranking stays a hypothesis

Every ranking above rests on evidence from other populations, so Prophylaxis must test it on its own users without breaking its privacy promise. Four mechanisms fit a static site with no server. First, **local-only metrics shown to the user**: rate of each habit per game over time, success on the second attempt at a resurfaced position, and practice streaks. These double as the progress feature that Harkin's meta-analysis supports. Second, **an opt-in, user-initiated export**: a small JSON summary of counts and rates, with no moves, usernames or games, that the user can read and choose to submit through a public form or GitHub issue. Third, **in-product acceptance votes** ("Was this reason right?") stored locally and included in the export. This extends the pin test into a standing quality check for every explanation type. Fourth, **offline evaluation on public data**: the owner's existing chess.com samples plus the CC0 Lichess database can measure explanation coverage and precision without any user data. A few moderated sessions with low-rated players thinking aloud would show which reasons they understand, something none of the sources measured.

The kill criteria should be set before data arrives. One option is to retire any explanation type that people accept less than a set share of the time, as the pin was retired at about a third. Another is to remove Compare or the opening tree if opt-in summaries show negligible use. A third is to treat a rating rise as meaningful only when the habit rate also fell, because regression to the mean and new-account rating swings will otherwise dominate. The exact thresholds are the owner's call. Committing to them in advance is what makes the process credible.

## Conclusion

The research changes the question from "which AI features should Prophylaxis add?" to "which facts can Prophylaxis state correctly that nobody gives free users?" The answer splits cleanly. The *what went wrong* belongs to Stockfish and rules, and the biggest remaining gain there is old-fashioned engineering: threat checks and short lines. The *how hard was it for you* belongs to Maia, the one ML model whose output is both novel to users and checkable against data Prophylaxis already holds. LLMs, chat and single-number ratings sit on the gimmick side for a $0, in-browser product, not because they are unfashionable but because the published error rates are too high for a beginner audience that cannot spot mistakes.

For the owner's career goals, the strongest portfolio artefact is not any single model. It is the visible decision record: a measured gap, features built to close it, labels removed when people rejected them, an estimator cut when it missed by about 300 points, and privacy-preserving measurement designed in from the start. Hiring managers for AI product roles see many "I added an LLM" projects. Few show the discipline to measure, prune and say no, and that discipline is what a reviewer will check for.
